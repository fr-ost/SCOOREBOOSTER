How to Write Replies That Authors Actually Answer (With Examples)

By Unique Labs · November 14, 2026 · 7 min read

An author scrolling their replies decides in a second whether yours is worth a response. Replies that get answered share one trait: they give the author something to do.

The test

Before posting, ask: could the author answer this in one line, and would they want to? If the only possible response is "thanks", the reply is decoration.

Six patterns that work

1. The sharp question

Ask about the mechanism or the edge case, not about the author.

  • Great thread! What do you think about the future?
  • does this still work once the incentives end, or is that the cliff

2. The implication

Say what follows from their point that they did not spell out.

  • So true, this is huge.
  • which means the teams with distribution win even with the worse product

3. The honest doubt

Disagree a little, with a reason, in a friendly tone. Authors answer doubts far more often than agreement.

  • I disagree.
  • fair, but doesnt this only hold if liquidity sticks around after the first week

4. The concrete example

Tie the point to a case everyone in the niche knows. Do not invent numbers or events.

  • This reminds me of many projects.
  • same thing happened with app chains, nobody cared until the fees did

5. The practical next step

Say what someone acting on the post should do first.

  • Very useful, thanks for sharing.
  • the first thing to fix here is onboarding, everything after that is noise until it works

6. The sharper restatement

Say their point in a more quotable way, with your own twist. Use sparingly; it is the easiest to do badly.

  • Exactly, well said.
  • boring infra wins quietly, then all at once

Phrases that make a reply invisible

These read as filler or as a bot, and many authors skim past anything that starts with them:

  • "Great post", "well said", "this is huge", "love this", "so true"
  • "Sounds like", "seems like", "looks like"
  • "Curious to see how this plays out", "excited to see"
  • "Thanks for sharing", "very insightful", "game changer"

ScoreX blocks these phrases outright and rewrites any draft that contains them.

Format and tone

  • One thought. Twelve to twenty-five words is usually right. A paragraph under someone else's post is a post of your own in the wrong place.
  • Plain and casual. Lowercase and loose punctuation are fine on X. Corporate polish is not.
  • No links, no hashtags, no tagging others. They look like promotion and X treats them with suspicion in replies.
  • Peer to peer. No flattery, no "sir", no asking for a follow.
  • Same language as the post.

When they answer you

Keep it going with one short message on what they said. If they asked a question, answer it plainly. If they corrected you, take it well. Do not restart the topic or repeat your first reply. Two good exchanges are worth more than ten first replies, and they are where follow-backs happen.

When not to reply

  • Posts about a death, a tragedy or personal bad news, unless you know the person.
  • Political fights. They attract the wrong attention.
  • Giveaways and engagement bait.
  • Posts where you have nothing to add. Skipping is a skill.

Using AI without losing your voice

AI is good at speed and bad at taste by default. If you use it, give it your own voice, forbid the filler phrases above, and read what it writes. We cover the details in AI Replies on X: How to Use AI Without Sounding Like a Bot.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a reply on X be?

One thought, usually 12 to 25 words. Short enough to read at a glance, long enough to say something specific.

Is it okay to disagree with a big account?

Yes, if you give a reason and stay friendly. A thoughtful doubt gets more answers than agreement does.

Should I use emojis in replies?

Rarely. One fitting emoji now and then is fine; rockets, sparkles and hundred-point signs read as promotion.
